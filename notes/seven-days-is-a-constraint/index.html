Notes

Seven days is a
constraint, not a
promise about
everything

Published 9 August 2026 5 min read Uncommun Logic

We deliver inside seven days of a signed brief. That is not a claim about how fast we type. It is a constraint we imposed on ourselves, and most of its effects are on what we agree to build rather than on how quickly we build it.

What a seven-day window forces

It forces the brief to be finite. A specification that cannot be delivered in seven days is a specification we have to cut, argue about, or refuse before we start. All three of those conversations are better on day zero than on day forty.

It forces reuse. Our catalogue is seventy-eight systems because building the same shapes repeatedly is how a two-person firm delivers in a week. Nothing on that list is novel to us, and that is the point. Novelty is expensive and you should not pay for ours.

It forces us to say no when we are full. A short window only means something if capacity is honest. See what we will not build.

It exposes fake requirements quickly. Under a one-week clock, features nobody can justify tend to fall out of the brief on their own.

What it does not cover

One category of work is not a seven-day job and we will not pretend otherwise:

  • Builds spanning several systems that all have to change together.
  • Anything involving a data migration, because the surprises live in the old data and nobody knows what is in there until it moves.
  • Work that crosses several teams, where the constraint is other people's availability rather than our build time.

When that is your situation, it is stated in the forty-eight hour document, with its own timeline, before you commit to anything. You will never discover it on day eight. A deadline you learn about after signing is not a deadline, it is a renegotiation.

The clock starts at the signature

Seven days runs from the day you sign the brief, not from the first conversation. Two things sit before it: the hour, and the forty-eight hours we take to cost the return. Neither is billable and neither is on your clock.

What is on your clock is the brief itself. If the brief needs your data, your access or a decision from someone who is on leave, the seven days waits for it. We put those dependencies in the brief by name so the wait is visible instead of arguable.

Why not longer

Because the alternative is worse. A four-week project has three weeks in which your priorities can change, your staff can turn over and the business case can quietly stop being true. Short delivery is not a speed feature; it is a way of reducing the chance that we finish building the right thing at the wrong time.

The short version

The seven-day delivery window mostly changes what we agree to build, not how fast we type. Here is what it forces, the one category of work it does not cover, and when the clock actually starts.
